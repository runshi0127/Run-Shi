From a random start, the robot must orient itself, follow tape to the hog line, launch three pucks, and come back to reload — all within two minutes.

The key idea? Let gravity do the launching: store the pucks high on a second level and release them down a brachistochrone slide, so a single servo handles both storage and launch.

The challenge

What does the robot have to do, and with what?

Two robots compete head-to-head on a curling arena. Ours starts from a random orientation in the waiting area, must find its heading, follow the black tape toward the scoring rings, launch 8-oz pucks before the hog line, and return to base to reload — with no remote control and only limited navigation cues.

Finding the way

How does the robot get from the waiting area to the hog line and back?

With only the walls and the black tape as cues, we planned a route the robot can follow reliably every run. Everything else in the design follows from it:

  1. Orient — rotate until the left and rear ultrasonic pairs read equal distances, so the robot is square to the walls.
  2. Find the tape — drive forward until the rear IR sensor crosses the tape, then turn right until the front-right IR finds the center line.
  3. Follow & shoot — the three middle IR sensors steer along the line; when both front IR sensors see the hog line, the servo releases all three pucks.
  4. Return & reload — reverse along the line until the rear ultrasonic reads under 10 cm, turn to face the start zone, and wait to reload.

System design

How do we turn the route into a working robot?

The route above breaks the robot into four subsystems, each owning one part of the task:

  • Locomotion — a four-wheel omni-drive that moves forward, sideways, and rotates in place to orient, follow the tape, and return.
  • Sensing & navigation — ultrasonic sensors find the heading from the walls; IR sensors follow the tape and trigger each state change.
  • Puck storage — carries three 8-oz pucks and releases them one at a time.
  • Launching — gives each puck enough speed to travel from the hog line into the rings.

All of them had to share a 13 × 13 in footprint and one battery, which drove the first and biggest design decision: the robot’s layout.

Why two levels?

How do we fit everything into 13 inches — and still launch a puck?

There is no room on one level. The 13 × 13 in footprint has to hold four drive motors, ten sensors, the electronics, the puck storage, and a launcher. Spreading them out was impossible, so the robot had to grow upward.

Height is also our energy source. We tested whether a puck released from the hog line could reach the center scoring zone using gravitational energy alone — and it could, as long as it started high enough. That made the upper level the natural place to store the pucks.

But the robot must stay stable. To keep the center of mass low, only the puck storage and its servo sit on the upper level. The drivetrain, sensors, wiring, and electronics all stay on the lower level.

Testing the flywheel — and dropping it

The two-wheel flywheel looked the most controllable, so we laid it out at the front of the chassis and built a prototype. At 500 rpm the puck gained very little speed: the wheels spun too slowly, and the rigid wheels could not deform to grip it. In parallel we tested the potential-energy plan — and simply dropping the pucks from the upper level down a slide gave them enough energy on their own. The flywheels, with their extra motors, weight, and current draw, were removed.

The fastest slide

Which curve loses the least energy on the way down?

With gravity as the only energy source, every bit lost in the slide matters. Instead of a straight ramp, we shaped the slide as a brachistochrone — the curve of fastest descent. The puck picks up speed early, reaches the bottom in the least time, and exits tangentially with no vertical bounce.

We plotted the curve with a MATLAB script and modeled the slide in Fusion 360 to follow it exactly. The exit energy combines the servo’s push with the 10-inch drop:

Etotal = ½ m vservo2 + m g hslide

Sensing the tape

How does the robot know where it is on the route?

Six IR sensors under the chassis read the black tape: a central triple array keeps the robot on the line, a front pair spots the hog line, and a rear sensor handles reversing and finding the tape after leaving the waiting area.

Each tape crossing is also an event for the state machine — it is what moves the robot from orienting, to line following, to shooting, to returning. Four ultrasonic sensors on the left and rear faces complete the picture by measuring wall distances for orientation.

Electronics

How is everything powered and wired?

An Arduino Mega 2560 runs the robot from a 7.2 V NiMH battery. The battery rail feeds the Arduino and the servo, and a step-up converter supplies 12 V to two L298N H-bridges for the four drive motors. Because battery voltage sags during a run, the motor PWM is voltage-compensated from a battery reading, and a short soft start avoids inrush spikes. Results & demo

Does the whole sequence hold together?

The robot completed the full autonomous run — orientation, line following, puck dispensing, and return — within the 2-minute limit, and ranked 5th out of 20 teams in the final checkoff.
